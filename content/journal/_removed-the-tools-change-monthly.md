There is a particular kind of exhaustion that comes from watching the design and AI landscape right now. Not the exhaustion of learning something hard, which is a good exhaustion. The other one: the feeling of having read forty announcements and understood nothing you could act on.

Every week brings a model that reasons better, a tool that generates the thing you used to draw, a plugin that promises to collapse a week of work into an afternoon. Some of that is true. Most of it is true only in the demo. The gap between the two is where the interesting work happens, and almost nobody writes about the gap, because writing about the gap requires having actually used the thing on a project with a deadline and a client who will notice if it doesn't hold.

This journal is about the gap.

## What we're actually doing here

VeraCruz works on problems that cross product, service, and physical. That crossing is the whole point of the studio, and it has a side effect: we end up testing tools under conditions most tool reviews never cover. A generative model that produces a beautiful interface concept is one thing. The same model asked to hold a service blueprint consistent across twelve touchpoints, six of which are physical objects and two of which are a person in a room, is another thing entirely. The second case is where a tool either earns its place in the studio or quietly disappears.

So the material for this journal is already being produced. It just usually stays in the studio — in project notes, in a Slack thread, in the shared understanding of a small team that has now tried something four times and knows what it's for. Publishing it costs us almost nothing and might save someone else a week.

## Two formats, one standard

Some things deserve three hundred words. We tried a tool on a real piece of work, here is what it did well, here is where it broke, here is whether we'd reach for it again. Those are **notes**, and they will be the bulk of what appears here.

Some things need longer. A pattern shows up across three unrelated projects. A way of working that seemed obvious turns out to be wrong. A question about what design work even is when a significant part of the production can be delegated. Those are **essays**, and they will be rarer, because they should be — an essay published before the thinking has settled is just a note wearing a suit.

The standard is the same for both: we only write about things we have used, on work that mattered, where being wrong would have had a cost. No roundups of tools we read about. No predictions we won't be held to. If a piece here can't point to something real behind it, it doesn't get published.

## Why publish at all

Three honest reasons.

The first is that writing forces a kind of precision that conversation lets you skip. "That tool is pretty good for early concepts" survives a chat. It does not survive being written down, because writing it makes you ask: good compared to what, at which stage, and what happened the time it wasn't? The thinking improves under the pressure of having to be legible to a stranger.

The second is that this is what a studio's expertise looks like from the outside. Not a case study written eighteen months after the fact, sanded smooth by everyone who had to approve it. The working notes, closer to the moment, with the uncertainty still in them.

The third is that the questions we care about are genuinely unsettled, and we'd rather think about them in public than pretend we've resolved them. What does craft mean when the first draft is free? Where does judgment still live? Which parts of the process were always the point, and which were just the cost of not having better tools? We don't know yet. That's the interesting part.

## The rhythm

There isn't one, and we're not going to invent one. A journal that publishes on schedule ends up publishing to fill the schedule, and the filler is always obvious. This one publishes when something is ready — which, if the last few months are any guide, will be more often than we expect and less regularly than a content calendar would like.

If that suits you, the [RSS feed](/journal/feed.xml) is the reliable way to follow along.

Otherwise: what's here is what survived contact with real work. Take what's useful.
